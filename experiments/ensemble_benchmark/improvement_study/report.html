MIDI lead-sheet following: evaluation and findings

Exploratory study, 6 October 2026. All 75 annotated preferred performances were evaluated: 49 async and 26 sync songs. Forty-two fixed settings were compared, followed by alternate-take, initial-tempo and random-seed checks.

Main result: using bass/root evidence and changing the temporal tracker improves the original baseline. The fine-tempo beam reaches 91.41% exact-bar accuracy on async; the particle setting reaches 72.96% on sync at seed 0. These are different settings. The async catalog-tempo clock already reaches 99.87%, so the async result does not demonstrate a better follower than that control.

What is being measured?

The tracker receives MIDI events in time order, an untimed unfolded chord plan, meter, the correct initial position/start time, and an approximate catalog tempo. Later reference timestamps are used only to score its outputs. Every 0.2 seconds, its estimated position is compared with the annotated performance.

  1. Exact-bar accuracy: the percentage of evaluated 200-ms frames assigned to the correct occurrence of a bar in the unfolded plan. Bar 5 in chorus 2 is different from bar 5 in chorus 3. A 90% score means 90% of sampled time is in the correct bar; it does not mean 90% of bars are followed perfectly throughout.
  2. Within-two-beat rate: the percentage of those same frames with absolute position error at most two quarter-note beats. An estimate of beat 42.5 when the reference is 44 has error 1.5 and passes; an estimate of 41 has error 3 and fails. This is a tolerance-based position measure, not beat-onset detection F-measure.
  3. Mean absolute position error: average distance between estimated and reference positions, in quarter-note beats. Every frame contributes, including catastrophic errors. Smaller is better.
  4. Median absolute position error: the middle frame error within one performance. This describes a typical frame but can hide large failures; it is always reported with the mean. Summary tables average each song's median, rather than computing one pooled median.

For a simple error sequence of 0, 1, 2 and 9 beats, the within-two-beat rate is 75%, mean error is 3 beats, and median error is 1.5 beats. Exact-bar accuracy requires the actual bar labels and cannot be recovered from these four error magnitudes alone. Near a boundary, a small error can cross into the wrong bar; within a long bar, a larger error can still remain in the correct bar.

Reference limitation: the dataset supplies bar boundaries. Fractional beat position is linearly interpolated between those boundaries; individual beat onsets are not independently annotated. Exact-bar accuracy uses the bar labels directly. The beat-error measures therefore use an approximate reference for timing within a bar.

Aggregation: calculate each metric per song, then average songs equally within async and sync separately. Long songs do not dominate the corpus summary. Silence remains part of the position evaluation; the tracker must keep time when no useful pitch evidence arrives.

Chord recognition is a separate evaluation

The original benchmark also checks chord labels every 0.1 seconds. The primary label is root plus base quality: major, minor, diminished, augmented or the annotated suspension. It ignores sevenths, extensions, alteration flags and slash bass. For example, C7 and Cmaj7 both reduce to C major, while Cm7 reduces to C minor. A separate seventh-level analysis retains seventh type.

Candidate chord labels are restricted to those in the supplied chart. This measures chart-conditioned harmonic recognition, not unrestricted chord transcription. The saved chord metrics include exact root/quality accuracy, root-only accuracy, balanced accuracy (equal weight per reference chord label), coverage, tie rate, and fractional tie credit. Missing evidence counts as incorrect on valid chord frames; annotated no-chord regions and annotation gaps are excluded from chord accuracy. Ties use a fixed ordering after any algorithm-specific tie rule; fractional accuracy awards 1/k when the correct chord is among k tied predictions.

The new improvement study optimizes and evaluates position following. It does not claim that its 91.41% or 72.96% scores are chord-label accuracy. Batch runtime is recorded for reproducibility; it is not measured live MIDI latency.

Which changes helped?

The original common grid integrates tempo predictions with pooled harmonic scores. The stronger harmonic representation uses the identified bass track and assigns template weight 2 to the chord root, 1 to other base chord tones and 0 to other pitches. The beam retains several likely position/tempo paths; the particle filter retains continuous position/tempo samples and updates their weights from harmonic compatibility. Both are online, non-neural prototypes.

Internal selection check

For each target song, the setting can be selected using the other songs' mean scores. Selecting separately by known protocol chooses s06 for async and p02 for sync; selecting one setting across both protocols chooses s06. No per-song oracle selects the best result from that song's labels. However, later candidate designs were informed by this inspected corpus: this internal leave-one-song-out check is not an untouched external test or a trained adaptive selector.

What do the more accurately followed tunes have in common?

For the sync particle setting, agreement between the exported bass pitches and annotated chord roots is associated with better bar accuracy. The fraction of non-silent frames containing a bass root has Spearman correlation 0.593 with accuracy. The highest five songs have median root-onset share 41.1%, compared with 28.3% for the lowest five. Their base chord-tone shares are 67.4% and 56.8%, respectively.

These are descriptive, reference-dependent measurements made after tracking; the follower never receives them. They depend on transcription quality and annotation alignment. They do not establish causation, a genre rule, or a feature available to an online weighting model. Sparse bass can work well on async because the supplied fixed clock is already accurate; the same interpretation does not transfer to sync.

During sync solo-role sections, particle bar accuracy is 83.3% for trumpet, 79.8% for piano, 63.4% for bass and 52.3% for drums. The piano label describes the annotated solo role; no piano MIDI was supplied to the tracker. The role subsets contain different songs and inherit earlier tracking drift, so this is not a controlled comparison of roles.

What the study establishes, and what remains open

The experiments identify workable, better-performing baseline configurations and useful failure cases. They do not yet establish an adaptive evidence-weighting model, unrestricted repeat/jump handling, unknown-start alignment, or a real-time end-to-end accompaniment system. Yamaha- and Pardo-inspired components are adaptations within common experimental followers, not reproductions of the complete published systems.

The inputs are automatic transcriptions of bass and horns, with drum MIDI used only in designated timing variants. They are not clean piano performances. Bass track identity is supplied by metadata. The reference is an unfolded performance plan with a synchronized known start. Independent verification of the audio/MIDI/annotation time origin remains outstanding, so the empirical scores are provisional. Local mixture duration differences alone cannot establish an offset.

A useful next controlled study would verify this alignment, add matched no-harmony controls for the final temporal models, and freeze settings before testing new compositions. Only then should an adaptive weighting experiment test whether causal measures of ambiguity or recent consistency predict when harmony helps correct tempo-driven drift.
